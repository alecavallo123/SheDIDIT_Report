The Central Case

#SheFUND should not be understood only as a loan product. Its transformative value lies in the full process through which women prepare, articulate, defend, and reposition their entrepreneurial projects. The study finds that the program generates capability gains, strengthens confidence, supports entrepreneurial identity formation, and increases perceived legitimacy — and that these effects are visible not only among funded entrepreneurs, but also among women who applied, pitched, received feedback, or participated in the wider #SheDIDIT coaching ecosystem.

The most significant finding of this study is that these non-financial effects operate across the full applicant pool, not only among those who receive funding. This has a direct and actionable implication for program design: the quality of the process matters as much as the quality of the financial product.

Aim & Method

Research Questions, Sample & Approach

A qualitative, exploratory study of how #SheFUND’s process — not only its capital — shapes migrant women entrepreneurs.

Purpose & Research Questions

This study was commissioned by #SheDIDIT and supported by the European Microfinance Network to generate qualitative evidence of #SheFUND’s impact, not only on funded entrepreneurs, but on all women who participated in the application process. The study proceeds from the hypothesis that the structured journey of applying, preparing, and pitching for funding generates empowerment effects regardless of the financial outcome.

How does participating in #SheFUND’s loan selection and coaching process shape migrant women entrepreneurs’ skills, confidence, and perceived legitimacy?
How do these experiences differ between those who receive funding and those who do not?

Study Objectives

  1. Assess the impact of #SheFUND’s full loan selection process and coaching across the participant population;
  2. Compare the experiences of funded and not-funded entrepreneurs and identify learning and empowerment mechanisms across both groups;
  3. Assess how #SheDIDIT’s ecosystem influences entrepreneurial identity, perceived legitimacy, and business development practices;
  4. Generate actionable insights for #SheDIDIT and reflective observations for the EMN and the broader field.

Research Design

The study adopts a qualitative, exploratory design concerned not only with identifying program outcomes, but with understanding the mechanisms through which those outcomes are produced. In-depth semi-structured interviews were the primary method of data collection, supplemented by document review and process reconstruction through interviews with staff, coaches, and external stakeholders.

Sample

The study draws on interviews across three broad categories: funded entrepreneurs, non-funded entrepreneurs, and organizational stakeholders (staff, coaches, and the external funding partner). Participants were recruited through purposive sampling facilitated by #SheDIDIT, aiming for analytical variation rather than statistical representativeness.

Data Collection

Separate interview protocols were developed for each respondent group. Funded entrepreneurs were asked about their entrepreneurial background, pathway into #SheDIDIT, coaching and application experience, the pitch and selection process, and the use and meaning of the loan. Non-funded entrepreneurs and coaching participants were asked similar questions but with greater emphasis on the experience of not being selected, the quality of feedback, and continued connection to #SheDIDIT. Staff, coaches, and stakeholders were asked about program objectives, selection criteria, and opportunities for improvement. Interviews were conducted in English, lasted 40–70 minutes, and were recorded and transcribed with consent.

Data Analysis

Analysis followed an abductive thematic approach, combining deductive categories (business skills, financial literacy, pitch readiness, confidence, legitimacy, network access) with themes emerging inductively from the data (emotional safety, peer recognition, the experience of rejection, the importance of feedback quality). Within-case analysis reconstructed each participant’s journey; cross-case analysis compared patterns across funded and non-funded participants; stakeholder interviews triangulated findings against program design.

Limitations

This study has limitations linked mostly to the small sample size, and findings should not be interpreted as generalizable. The study relies primarily on self-reported experiences. For these reasons, the findings should be understood as analytical and explanatory rather than causal in a strict evaluation sense. Despite these limitations, the qualitative design provides rich insight into mechanisms — confidence, legitimacy, community, identity — that are difficult to capture through standard financial indicators alone.

Design & Loan Characteristics

#SheFUND was launched by #SheDIDIT in 2023, delivered in partnership with MicroStart, Belgium’s leading microfinance institution. It is not designed to serve any applicant — it is designed to serve women who are ready: those who have completed coaching, developed a business plan, and are prepared to defend their model to an external jury. No participant in the sample reported default or significant repayment difficulty at the time of interview.

Key Finding 1 of 6

Community Is #SheDIDIT’s Most Powerful Differentiator

Applies to all participants

Every participant, regardless of funding outcome, program phase, or national background, identifies belonging to a space that understands their intersectional experience as migrants, women, and entrepreneurs as the single most impactful aspect of engagement. Community is not a peripheral feature. It is the foundation that makes the rest of the intervention effective: the mechanism through which coaching becomes credible, the pitch meaningful, and non-selection survivable.

Community as Enabling Infrastructure

The most consistent finding across the interviews is that community is not a peripheral feature of #SheDIDIT’s work. It is the foundation that makes the rest of the intervention effective. Participants repeatedly describe #SheDIDIT not only as an organization that offers training, coaching, or access to finance, but as a space in which they feel understood, recognized, and taken seriously as migrant women entrepreneurs.

“I was rebuilding myself from scratch. Professionally, personally. All I was searching for was the right people. People I could trust. #SheDIDIT was foundational to my business.”

Many entrepreneurship support programs provide technical assistance, but fewer create an environment in which participants feel that their specific social position — as women, migrants, founders, mothers, newcomers, racialized entrepreneurs, or non-native speakers — is seen as part of the entrepreneurial journey rather than treated as an obstacle to be overcome individually.

“I also have to find the balance between being a mama and finding time for myself. In the last five years that didn’t happen too much.”

Community emerges as #SheDIDIT’s most powerful differentiator because it is a mechanism of trust formation. Participants are more willing to share doubts, disclose uncertainty, ask basic questions, receive feedback, and expose unfinished ideas because the environment is perceived as safe and non-judgmental. Women are not only learning how to structure a business plan — they are also learning how to see themselves as entrepreneurs, how to speak with confidence about their work, and how to claim legitimacy in spaces where they may not have previously felt entitled to belong.

“That feeling of not being alone at the top. It was very nice to have that help.”

Trust as a Mechanism of Participation

Community strengthens the effectiveness of coaching. Coaching is not received as business advice delivered from above, but as support embedded in a relationship of recognition. Participants are more likely to interpret feedback as constructive rather than discouraging, and more likely to translate it into action. The same applies to the pitch process: the public act of presenting a business idea becomes less intimidating when it is situated within a community that has already helped participants rehearse, refine, and believe in their entrepreneurial narrative.

“…with time, we just really became like, sort of buddies… we would check on each other.”
“It always felt so good to be understood, shared about our worries and getting also so much support and so much love from the community.”

Community Reframes the Funding Decision

For funded entrepreneurs, receiving support through #SheFUND is experienced not only as access to capital, but as a signal of trust and validation from a community that understands their journey. For non-funded participants, community made rejection more accepted and understood: not being selected is disappointing, but when embedded in a broader support system, it becomes interpreted as a moment of redirection rather than a final judgement on entrepreneurial worth.

“It also helped me to get resources to network with other people.”

This is one of the most important findings of the study. The value of #SheFUND is not tied only to the loan — it is produced through the whole process, and community is what allows that process to remain valuable even when the financial outcome is negative. Community is therefore not a “soft” outcome. It is a productive asset that enables capability development, confidence building, identity formation, and continued engagement.

Strategic Implications

#SheDIDIT should treat community as a core program component rather than an informal by-product of its work — deliberately designed, funded, staffed, and measured. This could include structured peer interaction before and after the pitch, alumni engagement, facilitated spaces for non-funded applicants, founder circles, peer mentoring, and community-based follow-up after the funding decision. Community is both #SheDIDIT’s most defensible differentiator and its most underleveraged strategic asset.

Key Finding 2 of 6

Coaching Value Extends Beyond the Funded Cohort

Applies to all participants

The #SheFUND coaching curriculum delivers genuine technical value — business planning, financial literacy, pricing strategy — but participants consistently point to something more transformative: a coach who listened without judgement, and a moment of recognition that made their ambition feel real. Critically, this value does not depend on being selected: non-funded participants continued running their businesses, sought alternative funding, and in several cases directly applied what they had learned to grow their activities.

The Relevance of Soft Skills

When asked what changed most through their participation, most participants do not first refer to a worksheet or a financial template. They refer instead to confidence, clarity, self-belief, encouragement, and the feeling of being listened to by their coach without judgement.

“Making a financial plan, you can learn that from tutorials. The most valuable thing is identifying when someone is struggling with self-confidence and helping them make the mental click.”

Confidence development builds entrepreneurial legitimacy

Many participants entered with ideas, experience, or sector-specific expertise but lacked the confidence to translate these resources into a coherent entrepreneurial narrative. Coaching worked not simply as knowledge transfer, but as a process through which women were helped to organize their ideas, name their value, and present themselves as legitimate entrepreneurs.

“I knew that I had the capacity, I had the knowledge… I just needed this bridge, and that was what my business coach gave to me.”
“I’m actually executing what my business coach told me, and it works. I am earning money from that.”

Soft skills as practical entrepreneurial capabilities

Confidence affects whether a participant asks questions, attends events, approaches partners, speaks to customers, applies for funding, or stands in front of a jury. These are not marginal outcomes — they are practical entrepreneurial capabilities.

“What I appreciated the most was the mental support, the psychological support. I was kind of lost. Just having a coach that could listen to me and reassure me.. that’s what I appreciated the most.”
“The most valuable thing… they make us feel we are not alone and everything is possible.”

Translating Vision into Concrete Business Decisions

Coaching helps participants move from broad ambition to practical decision-making — clarifying what they offer, who they serve, how they price, how they communicate, and how they present their business to others.

“All coaches are very encouraging, very supporting. It always felt so good to be understood.”
“She literally helped me through the macro level and the micro level, like how I could see myself from helicopter view and how this translated into my brochure, my services, how could I name them, my prices. So it was exactly what I needed.”

The Journey Builds Discipline and Accountability

Some participants had already been in business for some time but had not yet developed systematic routines around budgeting, pricing, planning, or follow-up. Coaching helped them formalize these practices, with accountability that is supportive rather than punitive.

“It was the first time ever when she asked me that I did budgeting… even if I was in business for two years, I never did that properly.”
“The coaches, like, oh, you haven’t booked this this month. What happened?…gently.”
“I have really struggled to… be consistent, so it’s really helped me… to keep me focused on my goal.”

Relatable Coaches Make the Journey More Credible

Participants valued coaches who were entrepreneurs themselves, who understood the practical realities of building a business. This relatability strengthens trust and makes participants more likely to act on the feedback they receive — and it extends beyond the funded cohort: both funded and non-funded participants described concrete gains in clarity, confidence, financial understanding, and pricing decisions.

“…for sure, it brings me a lot of courage… when somebody… appreciates you what are you doing.”
“The most important thing is the confidence that you get when people give you money, or invest time and effort in you.”
— #SheDIDIT Founder

One non-funded participant, for example, launched an administrative support consultancy and was generating revenue within a year of completing #SheMeansBusiness, following the plan developed with her coach. Non-selection did not necessarily mean the program had failed to generate entrepreneurial progress — #SheFUND builds entrepreneurial readiness across a wider group of women than the binary distinction between funded and not funded can capture.

Strategic Implications

#SheDIDIT should communicate #SheFUND not only as a funding opportunity, but as a structured entrepreneurial learning journey, where each stage has a clear learning function: application as self-reflection, coaching as clarification and accountability, pitching as confidence building, feedback as redirection, and follow-up as continued engagement. #SheDIDIT should measure outcomes produced during the journey itself — confidence, pricing clarity, budgeting practice, business communication — not only loans disbursed or revenue growth.

Key Finding 3 of 6

Feedback Quality Shapes Applicant Outcomes

Centers on non-funded applicants

For non-funded applicants, the quality of feedback after the pitch determines whether the experience empowers or erodes. When feedback is substantive, aligned with evaluation criteria, and delivered with care, non-selection can redirect and motivate. When feedback is light, or when criteria are misaligned with what was actually evaluated, the process fails its own stated values.

The Pitching Experience

The pitch is not merely the end of the application process — it is a developmental threshold. It marks the moment a business idea moves from private aspiration into a public entrepreneurial claim, strengthening both the business proposition and the entrepreneur’s ability to inhabit the role of founder.

“I pitched, and the reaction was... wonderful... how well it really clicked with me and how they loved the product.”
“Before then I was pitching mostly to male audiences and it just did not relate so well. At this event, the vibe was amazing. The audience was wonderful.”

The persistence of these gains depends heavily on how the post-decision phase is managed. When non-selection is communicated constructively, it can become a moment of redirection. When it is communicated weakly, it risks undermining the confidence built during earlier stages.

Feedback as a Design Variable

For non-funded applicants, the feedback moment is one of the most important points in the entire #SheFUND journey. Feedback quality should be understood as a central program design variable, not a minor operational step.

“A no doesn’t mean that it’s not good. It just means that maybe... this is not the right place for you.”

Participants value feedback most when it is specific, actionable, and connected to the selection criteria:

“I was able to present the pitch. And she told me what could be improved, and what was good already, which I really appreciate.”
“I took consideration of some of the points and have improved it.”
“feedback is gold.”

But experiences were uneven. Several participants described feedback that fell short:

“I expected the jury to already know our businesses... I was a little bit disappointed... the feedback... was quite light.”
“I think I did not receive a systematic feedback about the pitch. I think I would, if I asked for it. Maybe I should have.”
“I didn’t get any feedback… I would like to hear like this went well or didn’t go well, or you should say more about that or that.”
“Maybe it would be good to meet one more time, or to have a like sort of results session where maybe we could hear that kind of feedback… I imagine contestants who didn’t win that they could hear why.”

This does not require feedback to be lengthy or resource-intensive. A simple template distinguishing strengths, areas for improvement, jury concerns, and recommended next steps would ensure all applicants receive comparable guidance.

Strategic Implications

If non-funded participants are considered part of the program’s impact population, feedback becomes one of the main tools through which that impact is sustained. Feedback should be formalized as a core component of the #SheFUND journey: timely, criteria-based, constructive, and oriented toward next steps. If impact reporting focuses only on funded entrepreneurs, the organization risks systematically undercounting its own value creation.

Key Finding 4 of 6

The Loan Funds What Coaching Alone Cannot

Based on the 3 funded entrepreneurs interviewed

Physical-product businesses face a hard financial floor before they can generate any revenue: minimum order quantities, tooling, packaging, and samples. Suppliers and manufacturers rarely offer gradual payment, so founders need a fixed sum committed upfront, regardless of how well the business plan has been prepared. The loan supplies exactly this kind of threshold capital that coaching alone cannot substitute for.

From Readiness to Execution

For product-based entrepreneurs, the first barrier is often not demand, motivation, or strategic clarity — it is that suppliers and manufacturers require minimum order quantities, production commitments, samples, packaging, testing, or equipment before the business can begin selling. A founder may know exactly what needs to be done, but still be unable to proceed because the required investment comes before revenue.

“You get some quotas of minimum orders that you have to make, and you also have to create samples and you have to do testing... If you don’t have the money, the fund, then it’s not possible... you need to have that money upfront once you start the process so you can advance. So if you don’t have that, then it stops there.”

The issue is not a lack of preparation, but the indivisibility of certain business costs. A minimum production run cannot be purchased one unit at a time.

“I bought a vacuum machine... a label machine... I also ordered ten thousand stickers for the jars. Those things were never money you have lying around when you are a complete person with a house, bills, and kids to pay for.”

For a third entrepreneur, the loan replaced a more expensive or less accessible financing route:

“That’s something I would have to take a loan elsewhere. I can pay it with this kind of funds.”

Strategic Implications

#SheFUND’s value for product-based businesses should be measured partly in terms of the specific, otherwise difficult-to-fund investments it enables: equipment, inventory, packaging, samples, testing, minimum production runs. In some cases, the most immediate outcome of the loan is not rapid growth, but the ability to produce, package, test, or launch at all. This suggests #SheFUND may be especially valuable for founders in sectors where early costs are lumpy and unavoidable — food, fashion, crafts, cosmetics — and points to a design opportunity: distinguishing upfront production capital needs (product-based founders) from visibility/certification/client-acquisition needs (service-based founders).

Key Finding 5 of 6

The Loan Functions as Protected Capital

Based on the 3 funded entrepreneurs interviewed

For founders who are also primary caregivers or sole earners, every business expense competes directly with rent, bills, and family needs — a tension that can quietly stall investment. Because the loan is earmarked and repaid on a predictable schedule, it functions as ring-fenced money that does not have to be justified against the household budget, removing a recurring source of financial guilt and hesitation.

Money That Does Not Compete with the Household Budget

Some founders are not only entrepreneurs — they are also primary caregivers, sole earners, or women carrying significant family and domestic responsibilities alongside their business. In these circumstances, business investment is rarely a neutral financial decision. The loan helps reduce this tension by creating a boundary between business money and household money.

“It’s not a present, because you have to pay back. But it’s a nice moment. You can order those things without sacrificing your own... I found it really nice, just being able to say, okay, I can buy those stickers and not cry about it, and I’m not going to have money to pay my electricity this month because I bought that.”

For small businesses, purchases like packaging, labels, materials, ingredients, tools, or small pieces of equipment may not appear large from the outside, yet for a founder with limited disposable income, each purchase can trigger hesitation. The loan reduces this burden by making the investment feel authorised.

Predictable Repayment Makes Investment Manageable

The loan’s repayment structure — spread over monthly instalments rather than a single lump sum — was itself what made investment feasible:

“It’s easier for me to pay back that amount monthly and being able to develop my product than not having, for example, already ten or twenty thousand euros that you have to give upfront yourself.”

This transforms a large upfront investment into a manageable monthly commitment, allowing entrepreneurs to act when the business needs it and repay gradually over time. The protection is not only financial — it is also psychological, protecting the entrepreneur from having to constantly justify each business expense against immediate family needs.

Strategic Implications

The loan should not be understood only as access to finance in a technical sense. It also acts as ring-fenced capital, protecting the business investment from being constantly compared with household needs. This insight also has implications for impact assessment: the loan’s first effect may not appear immediately in revenue growth, but in the entrepreneur finally feeling able to purchase equipment, packaging, or stock without fear of compromising personal financial stability. The loan may first produce confidence to invest before it produces measurable business growth.

Key Finding 6 of 6

The Loan Fills a Financing Access Gap

Based on the 3 funded entrepreneurs interviewed

None of the funded entrepreneurs interviewed described #SheFUND as one financing option they weighed against others. For migrant women without an established Belgian credit history, collateral, or professional network, conventional lenders are rarely a live alternative. The loan functions less as competitively-priced credit and more as the only accessible channel into formal financing.

The Only Viable Channel, Not the Cheapest One

For many early-stage entrepreneurs, and particularly for migrant women, the problem is not only the cost of credit — it is whether credit is accessible at all. Conventional lenders often require an established financial history, collateral, stable income, and administrative familiarity that recent arrivals in Belgium simply do not yet have.

“That’s something I would have to take a loan elsewhere. I can pay it with this kind of funds.”

The participant’s meaning is broader than it first appears: #SheFUND allowed her to avoid searching for, negotiating, or depending on another, less certain form of credit.

Credit Invisibility and Migrant Entrepreneurship

Founders who are new to Belgium may have professional experience, education, business ideas, and entrepreneurial motivation, but still lack the local financial history that lenders typically use to assess risk — a form of “credit invisibility.” #SheFUND helps reduce this mismatch by creating an alternative pathway for assessing readiness through coaching, application preparation, and pitching, rather than collateral or credit history alone.

Networks as Part of Financial Access

Access to finance is closely linked to access to networks. One participant described arriving in Belgium without a professional or entrepreneurial network:

“As a lawyer, as an expat here in Brussels, I didn’t have any network... I was just looking for places where I could learn about business, how I could actually launch this.”

Financial exclusion is not only about banks — it is also about not knowing where to go, whom to ask, which institutions to trust. #SheFUND embeds financing within a wider support environment, combining capital with guidance, community, and a trusted organisational relationship.

Strategic Implications

#SheFUND’s impact should be reported not only as the provision of credit, but as the creation of access where access would otherwise be limited or absent. For many funded entrepreneurs, the counterfactual is not a cheaper or more expensive commercial loan — it is no suitable financing at all. The programme’s value is therefore not only economic, but institutional: it helps founders become visible, credible, and financeable within the local entrepreneurial ecosystem.

Beyond the Six Findings

Additional Insights: Demand at the Edges of the Journey

Growing demand for #SheDIDIT support both before formal coaching begins and after graduation.

Beyond the six core findings, the interviews point to two distinct but connected gaps: one before formal coaching begins, among women interested in entrepreneurship but not yet ready for a structured program; and one after graduation, among women who have already developed their businesses through #SheDIDIT and now face more complex growth challenges. These insights directly inform Recommendations 3–5.

The Need for Earlier Entry Points

Some women arrive with ideas, ambitions, or early informal activities that are not yet ready for the intensity of #SheMeansBusiness or the formal requirements of #SheFUND. Several accounts point to long, non-linear entrepreneurial trajectories in which women carry an idea for years before they are able to act on it.

“…when you want to start in the track, you already have your idea… you need an idea. You don’t have an idea, it’s like no go.”
“they feel very unsure about themselves. Sometimes they just started and they haven’t given their business idea enough thought and… enough time to develop even a little bit.”
— #SheDIDIT coach
“I was thinking, it’s just weird that I would just start my own product… can I do it? It’s just funny, funny idea. But yeah, little by little, I just got crazy about that idea” — and lacked any entry point: “as a lawyer, as an expat here in Brussels, I didn’t have any network… I was at home with kids… I was just looking for places where I could learn about business.”

A lighter, more exploratory pre-coaching pathway — introductory workshops, peer circles, business idea clinics, confidence-building sessions — could bridge inspiration and structured entrepreneurship support, strengthening the pipeline into #SheMeansBusiness and #SheFUND.

The Need for Post-Loan / Scale-Up Support

At the other end of the journey, women who have grown through #SheDIDIT eventually face questions related to hiring, legal structure, investor relations, market expansion, and growth strategy — qualitatively different challenges from the ideation or early-launch stage. This creates a “cliff edge” after graduation.

“As a community person, to be honest, … I wanted to be in contact with people making it.”

Without a structured alumni or growth pathway, the organization risks losing contact with precisely the entrepreneurs who could become its strongest success stories, mentors, and ambassadors. The coaching waiting list is the clearest organizational signal that current demand exceeds available capacity — and a compelling argument for investment in program growth at both pressure points.

These two gaps together suggest #SheDIDIT could benefit from articulating its work as a staged entrepreneurial pathway: pre-coaching entry → #SheMeansBusiness → #SheFUND → post-graduation/alumni support.

Comparative Analysis

Funded vs. Non-Funded Participants

Where experiences converge, and where the funding decision creates different trajectories.

The programme produces value for both groups. Not-funded participants do not leave the process empty-handed — many describe the coaching, pitching, and community experience as meaningful in itself. At the same time, funding changes the depth and duration of programme impact: funded participants receive not only capital, but also institutional recognition, continued visibility, and stronger integration into the #SheDIDIT ecosystem.

Convergences

Both groups describe #SheFUND as more than a funding mechanism. Across outcomes, the programme is experienced as a process of entrepreneurial recognition, preparation, and belonging that moves participants from isolation toward visibility, from uncertainty toward articulation, and from informal ambition toward a more recognised entrepreneurial position — regardless of the funding decision.

Divergences

The main divergence is not simply the presence or absence of financial support, but what the programme becomes for participants after the selection moment. For funded entrepreneurs, #SheFUND becomes a platform for continuation: material support, symbolic recognition, and ecosystem access reinforce one another. For not-funded participants, the same gains are less likely to be consolidated if the participant exits the active programme environment after the decision — a difference in the durability of impact, not its presence.

Recommendations

Recommendations for #SheDIDIT

Six recommendations follow directly from the evidence, each with concrete actions.

1. Name Community as a Core Program Output

Community is the program’s most powerful differentiator and most consistently valued feature across every participant group — the single most cited element across all seventeen conversations in this study. It should be named, budgeted, and measured as a primary output rather than an incidental benefit.

  • Schedule regular community events as a fixed program feature rather than as add-ons dependent on available capacity.
  • Create peer cohorts within coaching rounds so participants who go through #SheMeansBusiness together maintain connection after graduation.
  • Develop alumni touchpoints that keep funded and not-funded graduates connected to the #SheDIDIT community over time.
  • Reinforce community spaces, both digital and physical, as part of the #SheHub offer.
  • Include a community engagement metric in #SheDIDIT’s impact reporting framework.

2. Standardise and Strengthen #SheFUND Feedback for All Applicants

Feedback to not-funded applicants must be aligned with the published evaluation criteria, structured, and sufficient for an applicant to understand specifically what would need to change for a successful future application.

  • Brief jury members with a review of all written applications before the pitch event.
  • Provide jury members with a structured feedback template aligned to published evaluation dimensions.
  • Offer a 30-minute follow-up call to all not-funded applicants within two weeks of the decision.
  • Ask not-funded applicants to rate the quality and usefulness of their feedback via a post-decision survey.
  • Review feedback quality annually and use findings to improve jury briefing.

3. Address the Coaching Capacity Gap Structurally

The coaching waiting list is a fundraising asset: unmet demand, quantified and reported, makes a compelling case for investment. Both aspects should be managed deliberately.

  • Introduce group coaching formats for early-stage participants as an interim measure.
  • Pilot peer-to-peer coaching circles facilitated by #SheDIDIT coaches.
  • Develop a digital self-paced onboarding resource for the waiting period.
  • Systematically collect and report waiting list data as evidence of demand to funders.
  • Model the fundraising case using waiting-list numbers and the estimated value of delayed support.

4. Develop a Pre-Entrepreneurial Pathway for Adults

#YouthPreneurs serves this function for ages 15–28; there is no equivalent lighter-touch entry point for adults not yet ready for #SheMeansBusiness. This gap is both a missed opportunity and a pipeline constraint.

  • Design a session-based pre-entrepreneurial program focused on values clarification, skills mapping, and idea generation.
  • Position it explicitly as a pathway into #SheMeansBusiness with clear progression criteria.
  • Use peer facilitation and group formats to keep delivery costs low.
  • Pilot with a small cohort, evaluated with the same impact framework as #SheMeansBusiness.
  • Consider partnerships with community organisations already reaching the target population.

5. Create a Scale-Up Support Offer for Established Entrepreneurs

The program currently ends when an entrepreneur is no longer a pre-starter or early-stage founder. For the strongest profiles — those navigating hiring, investor relations, and market expansion — this is a significant opportunity to continue supporting alumni.

  • Design an alumni coaching/mentoring tier for graduates in a growth phase.
  • Map specialist advisors in the #SheDIDIT network and formalise a referral pathway.
  • Explore partnerships with Belgian and European accelerator programs.
  • Host an annual alumni event connecting graduates to each other.
  • Collect data on post-graduation trajectories to build the evidence base for this offer.

6. Make Psychological and Mindset Support an Explicit Feature of Coaching

The psychological dimension of coaching — the validation, the identity work, the moment a coach says “this is real and you can do it” — is consistently identified as the most transformative element of the program, yet currently operates as an implicit, unmarked feature.

  • Include psychological support explicitly in #SheDIDIT’s description of the #SheMeansBusiness offer.
  • Train coaches to name the psychological dimension of what they do for participants.
  • Include a wellbeing and confidence measure in pre- and post-coaching evaluation.
  • Use participant accounts of psychological impact in external communications and funding applications.
  • Consider whether a partnership with a wellbeing provider would strengthen capacity for deeper needs.

Zooming Out

Implications for the Field

What the #SheFUND case suggests for the European Microfinance Network and gender-responsive program design more broadly.

For the European Microfinance Network

The impact of financial inclusion programs for migrant women entrepreneurs cannot be adequately captured by loan disbursement rates, repayment performance, or business survival statistics alone. EMN is well positioned to advocate for holistic impact measurement frameworks across its membership, and to actively promote the #SheDIDIT model as a replicable non-financial-services innovation: coaching-as-prerequisite, empowerment framing that begins with potential rather than disadvantage, a deliberately women-focused evaluation jury, and community-building infrastructure running through every element of the program.

A longitudinal follow-up study at 18–24 months post-#SheFUND participation, tracking both funded and not-funded cohorts, would answer questions this study cannot: does capability persist, does the business survive and grow, does the legitimacy signal translate into long-term institutional access?

Five Design Principles for Gender-Responsive Financial Inclusion

1

Begin with potential, not disadvantage.

Programs that start from strength and ambition produce different participant experiences than those framed around vulnerability and exclusion. #SheDIDIT’s commitment to treating migration background as capital rather than deficit shapes every interaction.

2

The process is the product.

The selection and feedback process is itself an empowerment intervention. Invest in its quality as seriously as in the financial product — a poorly executed process does not merely fail to empower, it may actively disempower.

3

Audience composition matters.

Pitching to a women-focused, entrepreneurially literate jury is qualitatively different from pitching to a mixed or male-dominated panel. Be deliberate about who evaluates.

4

Non-financial services and financial access are not alternatives.

Coaching and community produce capability and identity effects that financial access alone cannot generate. Design the two together, not as separate tracks.

5

Community is infrastructure.

Belonging to a space that understands one’s intersectional experience is the mechanism through which other program effects become possible. Build it deliberately, fund it adequately, measure it systematically.

Conclusion

#SheFUND Does Considerably More Than Provide Capital

#SheFUND does what it sets out to do: it provides interest-free capital to migrant women entrepreneurs who cannot access conventional financing. But it also builds capabilities through coaching and application preparation, provides a structured and institutionalised site for entrepreneurial identity development, confers legitimacy through a credible and transparent selection process, and connects participants to a community that understands, in a full and intersectional sense, what they are trying to do and who they are.

The most significant finding of this study is that these non-financial effects operate across the full applicant pool, not only among those who receive funding. This has a direct implication for program design: the quality of the process matters as much as the quality of the financial product. The experience of not-funded applicants deserves as much deliberate attention as the experience of funded ones, and the success metrics of the program should reflect the full scope of its impact.

Belgium’s entrepreneurial ecosystem does not yet reflect the diversity of its population. One in three Belgians has a migration background; a fraction of that proportion is visible in business ownership, investment networks, or entrepreneurial support ecosystems. #SheDIDIT was founded precisely to close this gap — not by treating migration background as a disadvantage to be managed, but by treating it as an asset waiting to be unlocked. The evidence from this study suggests strongly that the model is working. The task now is to scale it, fund it adequately, refine it where the evidence shows gaps, and hold it to its own high standards.
